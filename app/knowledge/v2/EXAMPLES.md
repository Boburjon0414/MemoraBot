# Examples

Few-shot demonstrations of the GOOD pattern versus distinct BAD failure modes. Match GOOD. Never produce any of the BAD patterns below — each one fails for a different reason, not the same reason repeated.

## GOOD — "kitchen" (easy concrete, A1)

```json
{
  "word": "kitchen",
  "meaning": "oshxona",
  "uzbek_pronunciation": "\"KICH-en\" — \"kichik\" so'ziga o'xshab eshitiladi",
  "mnemonic_story": "Onam oshxonaga kirdi-yu, juda kichik bir sichqoncha stol ostidan chiqib, to'g'ri idishga sakradi! Onam qattiq qichqirib, qopqoqni yopib qo'ydi. Kichkina mehmon esa idish ichida chiyillab, chiqib keta olmay qoldi.",
  "example_sentence": "My mother is cooking dinner in the kitchen.",
  "difficulty": "A1"
}
```
Why it works: "kichik" is a real, common word that genuinely echoes "KICH-en." It's staged as an actor causing a scream and a slammed lid, not mentioned in passing.

## GOOD — "cabbage" (harder concrete word, A2)

```json
{
  "word": "cabbage",
  "meaning": "karam",
  "uzbek_pronunciation": "\"KAB-ij\" — \"kabob\" so'ziga o'xshab eshitiladi",
  "mnemonic_story": "Oshpaz katta bir karamni to'g'rayotganda, hidi shunday kuchli chiqdiki, butun oshxona xuddi kabob pishayotgandek hidladi. Mijozlar burun tortib \"Kabob qayerda?!\" deb yugurib kelishdi — likopchada esa faqat yashil karam turardi.",
  "example_sentence": "She chopped the cabbage for the salad.",
  "difficulty": "A2"
}
```
Why it works: "kabob" (a common loanword) was chosen over a made-up chunk because it's a real word every Uzbek speaker knows — candidate evaluation matters more than raw phonetic closeness. The food-for-food irony (cabbage smells like kebab) doubles as the joke.

## GOOD — "confidence" (abstract noun, personified, B2)

```json
{
  "word": "confidence",
  "meaning": "o'ziga ishonch",
  "uzbek_pronunciation": "\"KON-fi-dens\" — birinchi bo'g'ini \"qon\" so'ziga o'xshab eshitiladi",
  "mnemonic_story": "Sahnaga chiqishdan oldin bolaning yuragi qattiq urib, qoni yuziga tepib, birdan ko'kragini kerib, boshini baland ko'tarib, dadil qadam bilan mikrofon tomon yurdi. Hamma hayron qoldi — bir daqiqa oldin qo'rqib turgan bola endi hech kimdan tap tortmasdi.",
  "example_sentence": "She answered every question with confidence.",
  "difficulty": "B2"
}
```
Why it works: an earlier draft of this example anchored to the idiom "qonim qaynayapti" ("my blood is boiling") — but that phrase leans toward anger or agitation in everyday Uzbek, not self-assurance, so it was replaced. "Qoni yuziga tepdi" ("blood rushed to his face") is a neutral, common description of a surge of feeling, keeps the same strong "qon" sound anchor, and the story stages confidence as a physical stance rather than defining it.

## GOOD — "hesitate" (verb, B1)

```json
{
  "word": "hesitate",
  "meaning": "ikkilanmoq",
  "uzbek_pronunciation": "\"HEZ-i-teyt\" — \"hozir\" so'ziga yaqin eshitiladi",
  "mnemonic_story": "Bola muzqaymoqni tanlashi kerak edi, lekin faqat \"hozir... hozir...\" deb g'udranib, qo'lini oldinga-orqaga tortib turaverdi. Navbatdagilar g'ijinib qarashdi, sotuvchi esa muzqaymoq erib ketguncha kutdi — bola hali ham qaror qila olmasdi.",
  "example_sentence": "He hesitated before answering the question.",
  "difficulty": "B1"
}
```
Why it works: verbs get staged as action, not described. "Hozir" (hold on/right now) is exactly what a stalling Uzbek speaker says out loud — the vowel match is approximate (a genuine Level 5 bridge, not a perfect one), but the anchor and the meaning reinforce each other so strongly that the looseness doesn't cost recall.

```json
{
  "word": "generous",
  "meaning": "saxiy",
  "uzbek_pronunciation": "\"JEN-er-us\" — \"jin\" so'ziga o'xshab eshitiladi",

```
Why it works: the dasturxon setting isn't decoration — endless, unstoppable giving is the literal meaning of the word, so the cultural scene earns its place instead of being pasted on.

## GOOD — "psychology" (long word, spelling-vs-pronunciation trap, C1)

```json
{
  "word": "psychology",
  "meaning": "ruhiyat ilmi",
  "uzbek_pronunciation": "\"sai-KOL-uh-ji\" — boshidagi \"p\" talaffuz qilinmaydi, \"-kol-\" qismi \"kollej\" so'ziga o'xshaydi",
  "mnemonic_story": "Kollejga yangi o'qituvchi keldi — u har bir talabaning miyasini ko'rish uchun maxsus ko'zoynak taqib yurar, kimning nima o'ylayotganini baralla aytib berardi. Talabalar hayratda: \"Bu odam fikrlarimizni o'qiyaptimi?!\" deb bir-birlariga qarashardi.",
  "example_sentence": "She is studying psychology at university.",
  "difficulty": "C1"
}
```
Why it works: pronunciation-first — the anchor is built on the spoken word (silent "p") not the spelling, chunked down to the one recognizable piece ("kollej"). The mind-reading teacher also stages the actual meaning (the study of the mind).

## GOOD — "influence" (abstract, weak phonetic match, Level 6 bridge, B2)

```json
{
  "word": "influence",
  "meaning": "ta'sir (o'tkazmoq)",
  "uzbek_pronunciation": "\"IN-flu-ens\" — kuchli tovush o'xshashligi yo'q; \"afsun\" (sehr) so'zi ma'no ko'prigi sifatida tanlangan",
  "mnemonic_story": "Sinfga notanish bir amaki kirib keldi-yu, birdan qo'lini silkib qandaydir afsun aytdi — shu zahoti hamma bola bir vaqtda o'rnidan turib, uning aytganini so'zma-so'z takrorlashga tushdi. O'qituvchi hatto o'zi ham to'xtata olmadi.",
  "example_sentence": "Her positive attitude influenced the whole team.",
  "difficulty": "B2"
}
```
Why it works: an earlier draft anchored to "gripp" (flu) with a claim that it shares a root with "influence" — that's simply wrong; "gripp" comes from French "grippe" and has no etymological link. Rather than paper over a bad sound-match with a false justification, this now honestly uses "afsun" (a real, common word for spell/charm) as a Level 6 semantic-assisted bridge: the phonetic tie is genuinely weak and the reasoning says so, but a cast spell — one person's words instantly changing everyone's behavior — is a near-literal staging of "influence," which is exactly what should carry the weight when sound can't.

## GOOD — "nervous" (neutral scene, culture *not* forced, B1)

```json
{
  "word": "nervous",
  "meaning": "asabiylashgan",
  "uzbek_pronunciation": "\"NER-vəs\" — \"nerv\" so'ziga o'xshaydi",
  "mnemonic_story": "Imtihon eshigi oldida navbat kutayotgan yigitning qo'llari titrab, u xuddi tokdan urilgandek bir joyda tura olmay yurardi. \"Nervlarim chidamayapti,\" deb pichirlab, ro'yxatni besh marta qayta o'qib chiqdi — javob esa o'zgarmagancha qolardi.",
  "example_sentence": "I always get nervous before exams.",
  "difficulty": "B1"
}
```
Why it works: "nerv" is already a natural everyday loanword ("nervlari buzildi"), and an exam waiting-room is universal — no bozor, mahalla, or to'y is needed here, and forcing one in would have weakened it.

## BAD — "kitchen" (dictionary definition disguised as a story)

```json
{ "mnemonic_story": "\"Kitchen\" so'zi \"oshxona\" degan ma'noni bildiradi. Bu so'z uy ichida ovqat pishiriladigan xona uchun ishlatiladi." }
```
Why it fails: no character, no action, no sound-alike woven in — a restated definition, forgotten by tomorrow.

## BAD — "opportunity" (invented word / meaningless syllable soup)

```json
{ "uzbek_pronunciation": "\"o-por-TU-ni-ka\" — maxsus yaratilgan so'z", "mnemonic_story": "Opportunika degan mavjudot paydo bo'lib..." }
```
Why it fails: "oportunika" doesn't exist in Uzbek — it was stitched together purely to match the English sound. No real speaker would ever say it, so it gives the learner nothing to hold on to. Rule 5 of the Sound-Association Engine exists specifically to prevent this.

## BAD — "generous" (story disconnected from the meaning)

```json
{ "uzbek_pronunciation": "\"JEN-er-us\" — \"jin\" so'ziga o'xshaydi", "mnemonic_story": "Bir jin uydan chiqib, bolalarni qo'rqitib qochib ketdi. Hech kim uni qayta ko'rmadi." }
```
Why it fails: the anchor is fine, but nothing in the story is generous — no giving, no abundance. A learner could recall this scene perfectly and still have no idea what "generous" means. Constitution Article 12 exists specifically for this failure mode.

Also avoid the pattern where every mnemonic defaults to a wedding, bazaar, or mahalla scene regardless of the word — see the "nervous" example above for what a well-chosen neutral scene looks like instead.
