# Uzbek Sound-Association Engine

How to find `uzbek_pronunciation` for any English word. Work through this internally; never show the process, only the result.

## Rule 1 — Pronunciation First, Never Spelling

Base the sound-alike on how the word is actually *spoken*, not how it is spelled. This matters most for words where spelling misleads: though, enough, thought, queue, psychology, subtle, debt, island, receipt, knife, comb. Say the word in your head first; anchor to that sound.

## Rule 2 — Evaluate Multiple Candidates, Don't Settle for the First

Before picking an anchor, mentally generate 2–3 candidates and choose the strongest one — not the first vaguely-similar word that comes to mind.

## Rule 3 — The Six-Level Hierarchy

Try these in order. Stop at the first level that produces something natural; don't force a lower level's complexity when a higher level already works.

1. **Exact/common Uzbek word** — a common word that genuinely sounds like the English word. Always prefer this.
2. **Colloquial everyday word** — a word or short expression Uzbeks actually use in daily speech (the kind of register as: aka, opa, bola, uy, non, choy, pul, bozor, mashina, tez, sekin — illustrative, not a fixed list).
3. **Short familiar phrase** — something a real Uzbek speaker could plausibly say out loud.
4. **Phonetic chunking** — split the English pronunciation into 1–3 memorable chunks and anchor each to a natural Uzbek word. Never more than 3 chunks; never meaningless syllable soup.
5. **Approximate phonetic bridge** — for hard words, accept a looser sound match in exchange for a much more natural, memorable Uzbek anchor. Naturalness beats precision.
6. **Morphological/semantic-assisted bridge** — when no direct sound-alike exists, pair a naturally-sounding Uzbek word or loanword with a semantic cue that ties back to the meaning (e.g. a word that shares a root or a borrowed-word cousin). The English word must still be retrievable from it.

## Rule 4 — The Naturalness Filter

Before finalizing an anchor, ask silently:
Would an ordinary Uzbek speaker recognize this instantly? Is it actually used in everyday speech? Could a person naturally say it out loud? Am I inventing a word that doesn't exist? Am I stitching together unrelated syllables? Would a learner still remember this anchor six months from now?

If it feels like "the AI built this word just to match the sound" — reject it and try the next level down.

## Rule 5 — Never Invent Fake Uzbek Words

A slightly imperfect but real Uzbek word always beats a perfect-sounding artificial construction. No exceptions.

## Rule 6 — When Nothing Fits Well (Difficult Words)

Don't panic and don't manufacture an ugly artificial phrase. Find the strongest natural fragment, pair it with a familiar anchor, and lean harder on the story to carry the memory. A 70%-accurate natural phonetic match wrapped in an unforgettable story beats a 95%-accurate match nobody would ever say.

**This system does not guarantee a strong sound match for every English word — it guarantees a natural one.** For a genuinely difficult word, the floor is always: the single closest real, everyday Uzbek fragment available, used honestly (even if the resemblance is loose), carrying the memory load through the story instead. That is a full, valid use of Level 6 — not a failure state. The only true failure state is inventing a word that doesn't exist. Degrade the phonetic precision as far as needed; never degrade into fabrication.
